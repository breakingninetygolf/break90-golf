Breaking 90 means shooting **89 or lower**. It does not mean making par on every hole, hitting every green, or suddenly finding another 30 yards with your driver.

On a par-72 course, **17 bogeys and one par add up to 89**. That's a useful starting point because it changes what a good decision looks like. A chip back into play can protect your round. A comfortable lay-up can be sensible. A bogey can be a perfectly acceptable result.

The next question is personal: where can *you* find the strokes? Start with your last three rounds, rather than a shopping list or a swing overhaul.

## The scoring math: what does it take to break 90?

On par 72, 18 bogeys total 90. Replace one bogey with a par and you finish at 89: **72 + 17 = 89**.

Several combinations get there. These examples contain no birdies:

| Pars | Bogeys | Double bogeys | Total on par 72 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 17 | 0 | 89 |
| 2 | 15 | 1 | 89 |
| 3 | 13 | 2 | 89 |
| 4 | 11 | 3 | 89 |

Each row covers 18 holes. You don't have to copy any of them exactly. They show that a double bogey does not automatically ruin a round, and that pars can create room for mistakes.

The target changes with course par:

| Course par | All bogeys | Maximum over par for 89 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 70 | 88 | +19 |
| 71 | 89 | +18 |
| 72 | 90 | +17 |

These are gross scores before handicap adjustments. Check the par on the tees you actually play. Our [bogey-golf strategy guide](/articles/why-bogey-golf-is-good-golf/) explains how to take this approach onto the course.

## Give each hole a realistic job

On a manageable par 4, a bogey could be a tee shot, an approach, a chip, and two putts. You can miss the green and still keep the hole under control.

On a long par 4, reaching the green in three shots and taking two putts also makes five. That may be a better plan than forcing a long approach over trouble with a club you rarely strike cleanly.

That doesn't mean laying up automatically. Look at the lie, carry distance, trouble, and the shot you'll have next. A lay-up into a bunker isn't a safe play just because you used a shorter club.

The aim is to give yourself ordinary next shots. Take a genuine par opportunity when it arrives; don't turn every hole into a test of whether you deserve one.

## Review your last three rounds

Use three recent 18-hole rounds as a practical starting sample. Five can help if your notes are reliable. Keep the course, tees, and conditions in mind: a windy round from much longer tees isn't directly comparable to a calm afternoon at your usual course.

Record the total score, but also write down what caused the expensive holes. If you only have totals, don't reconstruct precise mistakes from memory. Begin tracking them next time.

Copy this simple review into your notes:

| What to record | Round 1 | Round 2 | Round 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Course, tees and par | — | — | — |
| Gross score | — | — | — |
| Actual penalty strokes | — | — | — |
| Three-putts or worse | — | — | — |
| Extra shots after a failed chip or bunker escape | — | — | — |
| Trouble recoveries and what caused them | — | — | — |
| One decision you would change | — | — | — |

Count penalty **strokes**, not just incidents. A three-putt hole goes in the putting column once; note a four-putt separately. For short game, record a shot that left essentially the same task again, such as a chip that stayed in the rough or a bunker shot that didn't escape.

A recovery into the fairway is not automatically a mistake. Often it's the right response to an earlier shot. Note the cause instead of blaming the sensible recovery.

These categories are diagnostic notes, not formal strokes-gained statistics. They don't prove how many shots you should have taken from a particular distance or lie.

## Match the recurring problem to your next practice session

Look for a pattern across rounds. One terrible bunker hole matters, but repeated penalties or three-putts may give you a clearer first task.

| Pattern in your notes | What to investigate | One practical response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Penalties from the tee | Trouble inside your normal shot pattern; unrealistic carries | Choose a target and club that leave room for your usual miss; review [driver decisions](/articles/should-you-hit-driver-on-every-par-four/) |
| Long first putts followed by difficult second putts | Distance control and starting position on the green | Practice lag putts to a finishing zone; use our [three-putt guide](/articles/stop-three-putting/) |
| Repeated chips that stay short or run far past | Contact, landing spot and club choice | Practice one straightforward chip to a safe landing area |
| Failed bunker escapes | Contact and whether your setup fits the shot | Practice getting out reliably; consider instruction if the same failure persists |
| Multiple shots spent behind trees | Tee-shot target and attempted recovery | Plan the escape before choosing a club; favor an opening you can actually use |
| Approaches repeatedly finishing in trouble | Carry distance, club selection and target | Choose a larger safe area and check whether your usual carry reaches it |

Prioritize a problem that repeats **and** has a response you can practice. A golf lesson may be more useful than another drill if you cannot identify why contact keeps failing.

## Build a realistic bridge from your score to 89

Suppose your last three rounds were 96, 94, and 95. Your average is 95, so 89 is six strokes below that average. Six is the size of the gap—not evidence that six easy strokes are available.

Imagine your notes also show three penalty strokes and four three-putts per round. A useful first experiment might be to avoid one penalty stroke and turn one three-putt into a two-putt. If everything else stayed equal, that would move a 95 to 93. You would still need four more strokes to reach 89.

That's progress, and it tells you what to investigate next. Don't label every penalty or three-putt an automatic future saving. Some are difficult to eliminate, and another part of your game can have an off day.

**Don't count the same improvement twice.** If a safer tee shot avoids a penalty and also removes the need for a recovery, record one connected sequence. Don't add a separate, invented saving for every label attached to it.

You also don't have to find all six shots in one category. Better contact, fewer penalties, sensible targets, and one good putting day can combine. Your notes help decide where to start; they don't predict your next score.

## Take one focus into your next round

Choose one decision goal and one practice task. For example:

- **Decision goal:** Before each tee shot, identify the trouble your normal miss could reach and choose a target accordingly.
- **Practice task:** Spend a focused block on lag-putt distance control before your next round.
- **Review question:** Did those choices reduce the same problems you recorded in your last three rounds?

Use our [30-minute practice plan](/articles/30-minute-practice-plan/) to give that task some structure. Keep tracking the whole round, even if the first few holes go badly. Three rounds are a starting sample, not a verdict on your game.

## Can you break 90 with double bogeys?

Yes. On par 72, two pars, 15 bogeys and one double total 89. Three pars, 13 bogeys and two doubles also total 89. You need the complete scorecard to add up; you don't need a perfect stretch after every mistake.

## How many pars do you need to break 90?

There is no fixed number. One par and 17 bogeys work on par 72. On par 71, 18 bogeys make 89 without a par. Birdies and larger scores change the mix further.

## What should you track if you only have time for a few notes?

Start with penalty strokes, three-putts, and a short explanation of each double bogey or worse. Those notes won't describe every part of your game, but they can reveal a recurring decision or practice need. Add detail only if you can keep it accurate.

The target is 89. Your next task is smaller: find one repeated problem, make a plan for it, and see what changes over your next few rounds.
