A long first putt does not have to go in to be useful. For a golfer trying to break 90, leaving a comfortable second putt is a sensible practice goal. Start by giving distance control a measurable task.

## Set a three-foot finishing radius

On an appropriate practice green, imagine a circle extending three feet from the cup in every direction. The target is to finish inside that area. It is a practice zone, not a guarantee that every putt inside it will be holed.

Use removable markers only where the facility allows them, and keep your setup away from other golfers’ lines. You can also estimate the zone without putting anything on the green.

## Try nine putts from three distances

Choose starting points approximately 15, 25 and 35 feet from a hole. Hit three putts from each distance, using the same preparation routine. Count how many of the nine finish within three feet of the cup.

Then putt the remaining balls out and record the total number of three-putts. That second score matters: a useful lag still needs a committed short putt afterward. Repeat from the same positions so you can compare like with like.

## Change one condition at a time

Once you have a baseline, repeat on an uphill or downhill line. Record the change rather than comparing the score directly with a flat putt. A downhill 25-footer is a different task from an uphill one.

Keep your practice focused on pace and a clear start line. If you are experimenting with a technical stroke change, separate that task from the scored drill so you know what you are measuring.

## Use the warm-up to learn the green

Before a round, roll several longer putts and watch how far they finish beyond or short of your intended stopping point. Follow with a few short putts using your normal routine. The aim is to gather information and feel ready, rather than prove that you can hole every ball before heading to the tee.

On the course, consider the slope around the hole as well as the distance. Choose a pace and line together, then commit to that decision.

## Track the right outcome

Write down your three-putts after each round. When possible, note whether the problem began with the first putt’s pace, an uncertain read or a rushed second putt. Use that information to choose the next practice task.
